The concepts of folklore and algorithm, while seemingly antithetic, display some surprising resemblances: folk narratives, arts, and crafts are transmitted in time with little structural changes through consistent patterns, rules, and archetypes that resemble algorithmic logics. Similarly, the rules-based logical procedures that are commonly described as ‘algorithmic’ become implemented through lay theories, vernacular knowledges, and everyday practices that much resemble folklore. Beyond this parallel, the relationship between folklore and algorithms can be traced back to the development of an algorithmic culture that directly connects medieval mathematics to contemporary computational technologies (Striphas). Early examples of folklore about calculation include stories such as “The Chessboard of Sissah”, which describes how the inventor of the game of chess tricked an Indian king with the principle of exponential duplication, making him promise to double one grain of wheat for each square of the board (Bardi). Folklore about algorithmic processes also includes myths, legends, traditions and rituals: as Matteo Pasquinelli demonstrates through his analysis of the Vedic ritual Agnicayana, rules-based problem-solving thinking and practices “have been part of all cultures and civilizations” (28). In short, human history consistently suggests that when processes or methods of calculation become relevant for society, a folklore about them follows. 

With the advent of calculating machines and computational technologies, folklore about algorithms has expanded to the domains of computer software, machine learning, and artificial intelligence. Examples include software or coding in-jokes shared by programmers or hackers (Long et al.); urban legends about neural networks spreading via computer science debates (Branwen); and narrative templates that structure discussions about artificial intelligence systems (Woolridge 34). In a 2021 essay, researchers Ireti Akinrinade and Joan Mukogosi coin the term “algorithmic folklore” to illustrate how teenagers interacting with TikTok’s recommender system and trying to reverse-engineer it also engage in the production of strategic knowledge: “[b]y sharing experiences, asking questions, and crowdsourcing answers, teens are developing an algorithmic folklore while discerning the potential motivations behind TikTok’s software engineering”. In this definition of algorithmic folklore, the term refers to a quite narrow range of folk theories and vernacular knowledges developed in order to understand and potentially influence the functioning of algorithmic systems such as social media feed recommendations. 

Researchers working across disciplines have also arrived at this concept to define similar phenomena. Laura Savolainen, for example, examines the informal “beliefs and stories about algorithmic governance” (1096) through which Reddit users discuss the dynamics of platform moderation by constructing belief in the folk category of “shadowbanning” – a supposed moderation practice that hides certain content from a user’s audience without their knowledge. Minna Ruckenstein’s 2023 book, _The Feel of Algorithms_, based on extensive interviews with Finnish people about their encounters with algorithms, foregrounds algorithmic folklore as a key form of literacy to be taken seriously even when it appears to be grounded on folk explanations (55). Folklore scholar Guro Flinterud (454) theorizes how algorithms not only become part of folklore but also play a role in its production and dissemination. Gabriele de Seta makes a similar point by defining algorithmic folklore as the repertoire of genres and practices that emerge from encounters between vernacular creativity and computational automation: “algorithms are not just a topic of vernacular creativity, but become its technological medium and actively participate in the creation and circulation of content” (“An Algorithmic Folklore” 240). In contrast to computational approaches that utilize big data and quantitative methods to analyze folkloric repertoires (Tangherlini), algorithmic folklore offers a useful analytical category to understand and conceptualize the new kinds of explanatory narratives, beliefs and practices developing around and through algorithmic systems (de Seta, "Four Theses").
