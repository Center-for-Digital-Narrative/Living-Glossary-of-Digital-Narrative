An archetype, in its broadest sense, is an abstract category formed by setting aside the particular differences among its instances. In narrative, archetypes may be plots, themes, characters, or settings: the coming-of-age story; the conflict between individual and society; the hero or the trickster; the forest or the underworld (“Archetype” 48–49). 

The archetypal approach to narrative took shape at the turn of the twentieth century along two converging paths, the comparative anthropology of James Frazer and the depth psychology of C. G. Jung. In _The Golden Bough_, Frazer compared myths and rituals across societies, tracing patterns such as the sacrifice or symbolic renewal of a sacred king. His method then influenced the Cambridge ritualists, who read literature as a transformation of earlier ritual forms (“Archetype” 49–50). Meanwhile, for Jung, archetypes are not only recurring cultural images but part of the structure of a collective unconscious, repeating across cultures and epochs, and charged with what he calls numinosity: an emotional intensity that makes the image closer to a religious encounter than to a literary device (Symbolic Life par. 589). The king, the great mother, the maiden, the child, and the self recur because psychic life is shaped by transpersonal patterns that become visible through particular images. Jung distinguishes the “archetype-as-such” – an irrepresentable basic form that “is not capable of being made conscious” – from the varied, culturally inflected images through which it appears (_Structure_ par. 417). 

This account has been criticized as essentialist and universalizing. It risks treating contingent patterns as expressions of a timeless human psyche, flattening cultural difference, licensing interpretations through overly capacious typologies, and privileging a particular 20th-century European imagination. 

Later theorists retained the language of archetype while detaching it from a religious or psychic framework. Northrop Frye, for example, defines the archetype as a symbol that “recurs often enough in literature to be recognizable as an element of one’s literary experience as a whole” (99). The source of recurrence thus shifts from primordial forms to the conventions of literature itself, through which individual works can be located as part of a larger tradition extending back to antiquity. Archetype overlaps with several related concepts: the _trope_ is a familiar convention; the _masterplot_ is a repeating skeletal story blueprint; and _story grammars_ are recurring actions and roles, as in Vladimir Propp’s _functions_ of the Russian folk tale or the structuralist _actants_ (or typical roles) of seeker, helper, opponent, and so on. These concepts tend to explain recurrence through narrative structure rather than psychic universality. 

Yet the narratological search for underlying form depends on abstraction. Drawing on Marian Roalfe Cox’s 1893 study of 345 variants of Cinderella, Barbara Herrnstein Smith argues that the narratologist’s “basic Cinderella” is produced by stripping away the very features – the audience, the moment, the teller’s intention – that make any particular Cinderella a story at all. Some folklorists, Smith notes, came to suspect that if they continued long enough, every story would turn out to be a version of Cinderella, even _King Lear_ – a totalization that says more about the analyst’s procedure than about the tales (213–36). 

Digital narratives offer an unusually dense environment in which to observe archetypal patterns. Social-media platforms circulate recognizable personas and story forms with a speed and reach unavailable to earlier comparativists such as Frazer: the dramatic personal transformation, the persecuted truth-teller, the idealized domestic woman. The online “tradwife”, for example, resembles older figures of the devoted wife, fertile mother, or pastoral innocent. 

Generative AI makes archetypes even more explicit. From one angle, large language models (LLMs) appear to literalize the collective unconscious: a vast, collectively produced corpus from which recurring patterns can be retrieved and recombined, although the substrate is data rather than psyche. More precisely, however, generative AI resembles Frye’s account of literature as an interconnected system of recurring forms more than Jung’s account of a collective unconscious. From another angle, LLMs automate the abstraction Herrnstein Smith warns against: an LLM can generate a thousand further versions of _Cinderella_ on demand. 

These are not, in the end, competing analyses of stories but competing accounts of what it means for a pattern to recur, and thus of what reality looks like “beneath the surface” of historical particulars: between the universalizing approaches of figures such as Jung and Frazer and the more historically and contingently oriented approaches of Frye and Smith, though in different ways.
