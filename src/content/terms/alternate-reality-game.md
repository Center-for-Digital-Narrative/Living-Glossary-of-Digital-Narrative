Artist and researcher Jane McGonigal argues that “ARGs [Alternate Reality Games] are immersive, massively multiplayer experiences that unfold in the course of people’s real lives for days, weeks, or months” (McGonigal 13). As with every genre, the borders and exact features of an ARG remain fluent, even within the community, but the mixture between game world and reality is a consistent feature (Egenfeldt-Nielsen et al. 41). One of the other spearheads of ARGs, the creator of _The Beast_, Sean Stewart, describes the following features of ARGs: the story is broken into pieces that the audience must find and assemble; it is not bound by a single medium or platform, instead using text, video, audio, Flash, print ads, billboards, phone calls, and email to deliver parts of the plot; the audience is massive and _collective_, using communication technologies to work together; and players are not only responsible for exploring the world but can also meaningfully affect how the story progresses, taking a key role in creating the fiction (“Collaborating with the Audience”).

The immersive experience provided by ARGs, following the motto “this is not a game,” hiding the fact it being a game at all, is considered a main goal (Szulborski 1). This provides an otherwise barely possible blur between fiction and reality, everyday life and impossible adventure. This intersection of “lure and blur” is fulfilling “a thirst for authenticity coupled with a love of artifice” (Rose 15). Within the ARG-space one encounters multiple terms, e.g. “Puzzlemaster”, referring to the game masters, organizers and such; “Rabbit Hole”, representing the initial clue; “Breadcrumbs”, morsels of narratives, and “dead drops”, hidden caches of information (Davies 65). 

ARG’s history started as marketing campaigns, mostly for Hollywood cinema and video games - like the previously mentioned, _The Beast_, by Stewart and others. Considered as one of the first ARG ever, it promoted Steven Spielbergs’ 2001 movie _A.I._ (Kim et al.). In the game, people would follow specially created websites, film posters, film trailers, messages on telephones, and more to unravel the mysterious death of “Evan Chan” in the fictious world of _A.I._ in the year 2142 (Duggan 132). Other examples include “Why so serious?” created as part of the promotion for _The Dark Knight_ (2008), where players became part of the Joker’s heist (Meslow), or “I like Bees” a viral ARG promoting the release of the video game _Halo 2_ (_Avant-Gaming_).  

Entering the mainstream in the 2000s, ARG has never fulfilled the promising future its creators envisioned, staying in a niche market for the most part as of 2025. Nonetheless, the features and approaches outlasted the games itself, finding use in different environments. For example, the reoccurring aspect of the collaborative, gamified problem-solving approach of ARG’s make them interesting for learning applications. Studies show students foster their critical thinking as well as collaboration and problem-solving skills playing ARG’s (Gilliam et al.; Tornqvist and Tichon). This potential is realized on smaller scale in some public spaces like museums, where visitors and especially school classes learn content of the exhibition (Hsu and Liang; Liang et al.) 

On the flip side, the very intention to blur the lines contains dangers regarding ethics and limits of play, something discussed throughout academia and the public. While most creators of games reflect about limits of immersion and creating safe spaces (Davies, “The Gamification of Conspiracy: QAnon as Alternate Reality Game” 66; Davies, “Towards an Ethics of Alternate Reality Games”), the principles of ARGs were and are mirrored in more radical, political, and dangerous ways. As a popular research subject and a great example of said practices, the “QAnon” conspiracy theory echoes immersive game genres, combining them to become “all-encompassing” and “highly addictive” – the ultimate compliment for creators of ARG becomes political (Daly). The framework of ARG is being abused in QAnon, as the core principles of riddles, collective problem solving, as well as game-related and engagement (Davies, “The Gamification of Conspiracy: QAnon as Alternate Reality Game” 65).
