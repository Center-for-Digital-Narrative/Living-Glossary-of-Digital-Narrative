In his seminal 1961 work, _The Rhetoric of Fiction_, Wayne C. Booth mentions that stories do not take place by themselves, that narrators do the work of transforming an event into a story. Narration is present in every story, and thus, a narrator is present in every story. In the same work, Booth identifies that not every narrator is the same – that there are reliable and unreliable narrators. The latter is most often that “the narrator is mistaken, or he believes himself to have qualities which the author denies him” (Booth 159). A general example of a reliable narrator is the voiceover on a nature documentary; a general example of an unreliable narrator might be an influencer promoting a product as part of a brand deal, narrating a fabricated anecdote. The first hypertext fiction, _afternoon, a story_, famously has an unreliable narrator (Ensslin). 

Narrators who lie deliberately also fall under the term of unreliable narrator: a general literary example is murder mystery narrators who are revealed to be the killer and lie by omission for the duration of the narrative. In the digital space, this might be social media users who post AI-generated videos with the intention of passing them off as real. Users who troll and ragebait can also be interpreted into this category – gaslighting other commenters, for example, into believing they misinterpreted the original poster’s argument –  although it is difficult to track the genuine intentions of any user.  

But there are also narrators who do not realize their unreliability; this is the specific kind of unreliable narrator that Booth identifies in his definition. James Sunderland of the video game, _Silent Hill 2_, exemplifies this kind of cognitive dissonance. Like James, the player navigates and shares the experience of returning to the town of Silent Hill following a letter from his late wife, Mary, whom James narrates died of illness. The player only realizes later in the game, alongside James, that he killed his wife out of resentment and inability to cope with her disease (“What Silent Hill 2…”). James is “mistaken” in believing Mary succumbed to her illness for the majority of the game, until he is snapped out of his delusion by seeing a videotape of himself committing her murder – until the game (or the ‘author’) explicitly denies him the “quality” of sanity. 

While artificial intelligence and narratology have long been in conversation (Galloway; Hunter), recent narratological critique has turned to the specific inquiry of AI’s potential for generating narrative. Literature on narrative AI takes on a broad range of opinions: the generated, synthetic nature of an AI narrative can defamiliarize readers from the story via noting the rhetorical ‘deceptive’ moves made by the model (Caracciolo); LLMs’ inability to stray from text-centric analysis and utilize rhetorical analysis (via “tacit shared knowledge”) is what bars it from successfully recognizing or writing unreliable narration (Phelan, “ChatGPT” 204). 

The unreliable narrator is a complex narrative element that is still heavily contested within literary discourse. See the _Further Reading_ section for further recommended readings on narratology and the unreliable narrator.
