AI “hallucinations” occur as a side-effect of how AI programs work, as they are not grounded in any experience of the world but rather built to statistically predict words or detect patterns. Within the realm of AI, the term “hallucination” has lacked consistency in its usage and definition, as well as a precise and universally accepted definition (Maleki et al.). Alternative terms for “hallucinations” have been proposed by researchers both within and outside of the field, such as confabulations, delusion, stochastic parroting, factual errors, and fabrication (Maleki et al.). 

The first use of the term “hallucination” has been traced back to a dissertation in 1986 by Eric Mjolsness entitled “Neural Networks, Pattern Recognition, and Fingerprint Hallucination” (Mjolsness). In 2000, it was used positively in a paper for the generation of high-resolution enhancement of low-resolution inputs in face recognition (Baker and Kanade). In these early usages, “hallucinations” were seen as something positive and valuable and were used to describe the ability of computer vision programs to add details and denoise images, image inpainting, as well as image synthesis (Xiang et al.; Pumarola et al.; Baker and Kanade). 

Only later did “hallucination” begin to be used when describing errors, adapting the term for its analogous usage in language models. In a 2008 study on statistical machine translation, “hallucination” was used to describe a failure mode (Deng and Byrne). Later, in 2015, the term was used in a blog post by Andrej Karpathy—a founding member of OpenAI—to describe his recurrent neural network language model that was generating a false Yahoo URL and algebraic geometry (Karpathy). In 2017, Google researchers used the term to describe the responses generated by neural machine translation models that were “quite fluent but entirely unrelated to the input” (Koehn and Knowles). By 2018, usage of the term has begun to pick up in the domains of Natural Language Generation and LLMs (Maleki et al.).  

Since the proliferation of LLM-based chatbots, the term “hallucination” has gained wider recognition and usage beyond the discipline of computer science. After OpenAI released the beta version of ChatGPT in November 2022, users noticed that the program would often generate confident and plausible-sounding fabrications (Weise and Metz). In 2023, “hallucinate” was the Cambridge Dictionary’s 2023 Word of the Year, and the dictionary updated its definition of hallucination to include the AI-specific meaning of the term (Creamer). Dictionary.com also awarded “hallucination” as its 2023 word of the year, noting a 46% increase in dictionary lookups over the previous year (Dictionary.com’s 2023 Word of the Year Is… | _Dictionary.com_).

The use of the term “hallucination” as a metaphor, however, remains controversial and has been described as a “misnomer” since AI lacks sensory perceptions, and its errors arise from data and prompts rather than the absence of any external stimuli (Maleki et al.). It has been criticized for stigmatizing mental illnesses—particularly schizophrenia—and for further anthropomorphizing AI models by attributing human characteristics to them, likening AI “hallucinations” to the psychiatric phenomenon of false sensory perceptions that feel real but occur without any actual external stimulus (Østergaard and Nielbo). Using the term “hallucination” reinforces the metaphor of already treating AI systems and computers as if they were humans with brains and subjectivity, alongside ideas of intentionality, authorship, beliefs, and cognition.  

The metaphor of “hallucination” also invokes similarities with psychedelics, particularly LSD and psilocybin. In 2015, Google engineers developed a computer vision program called DeepDream that uses a convolutional neural network designed to find and then enhance patterns or faces in images, connecting it back to the original definition of “hallucinations”, adding details to images (Mordvintsev et al.). This program was used by the developers to falsely detect and enhance patterns and images via “algorithmic pareidolia”, thus creating deliberately overprocessed images that resembled psychedelic hallucinations (LaFrance). This additional use of “hallucination” once again suggests a resemblance between these models and the workings of the human brain. This can speculatively be connected to the Silicon Valley’s history of counterculture and recreational psychedelic drug usage (Tvorun-Dunn).  

When considering the criteria by which one judges AI “hallucinations”, if one is looking for accurate information, “hallucinations” are explicitly seen as errors. However, when used for more creative pursuits, their usage opens up new potentials. Going back to the original, more positive connotations of “hallucinations”, their ungroundedness can be used to create more details used for surreal visual storytelling, or “sympoietic creativity” (Ge; Halperin and Lukin).
